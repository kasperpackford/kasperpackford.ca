I wanted to understand Linux properly, so I stopped reading about it and built a box that other people depend on. It runs Ubuntu 24.04 on a Ryzen 5 4600G with 44GB of RAM, and everything on it is something I set up, broke at least once, and fixed.

Today it handles DNS-level ad blocking for the whole house, monitors itself, backs up my password vault on a schedule, and hosts game servers for a group of friends who notice immediately when anything is down. Remote access runs over Tailscale so nothing is exposed to the open internet.

What I learned the hard way

My nightly Bitwarden backup kept failing silently. The Bitwarden CLI is installed as a snap, and snap confinement meant it simply could not write to the path I had chosen under /opt. Nothing errored loudly, the backups just were not there. Moving the target into my home directory fixed it. The real lesson was that a backup you have never restored from is not a backup, so now I check it.

I learn things by running them, not by reading about them.

I graduated from the Computer Programming program at Algonquin College with Dean's Honours, and somewhere in there I figured out that I learn a lot faster when something is actually at stake. So I built a server, put things on it that people rely on, and became the person who has to fix it.

That is most of what I do now. When I want to understand a tool, I deploy it, break it, and keep it alive. It has taught me more about Linux, networking, and backups than any course did, mostly because a course never wakes you up to tell you DNS is down for the entire house.

I am looking for an IT or tech role in the Ottawa area where I can do that kind of hands-on work for a team instead of just for myself.
